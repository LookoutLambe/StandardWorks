Reading Hebrew

קריאת עברית

Hebrew is read right to left. In this interlinear edition, each Hebrew word appears above its English gloss; the Transliterate button adds a pronunciation under every word, and a tap on any word opens its root scorecard. This guide introduces the Hebrew alphabet and vowel system, the marks you will meet in this edition, and the reader itself, so you can begin reading on your own.

Nikkud (Vowel Points)

Biblical Hebrew writes vowels as small dots and marks placed above or below the consonant letters. These are called nikkud (literally "dotting"). The consonant is always spoken first, followed by its vowel.

The Dagesh (Dot Inside a Letter)

A dot placed inside a consonant is called a dagesh. For three letters, it changes the pronunciation:

בּ b (boy) ב v (vine)
כּ k (king) כ kh (loch)
פּ p (park) פ f (fire)

For all other letters, the dagesh simply indicates a doubled or strengthened sound.

The Maqqef (Hebrew Hyphen)

The maqqef ־ is a small horizontal line that joins two or more words into a single unit, much like a hyphen in English. For example: אֶת־הָאָרֶץ (et-ha'arets) = "[ACC]-the-land."

The Transliteration in This Edition

The Transliterate button writes each word in Latin letters. The scheme is the popular one, not the scholarly one: ch is ח (as in Bach), kh is כ without a dagesh (loch), ts is צ, k stands for both ק and כּ, sh and s follow the dot on שׁ and שׂ, and א and ע are not written. A doubled consonant shows a dagesh forte: lakkore for לַקּוֹרֵא. Words joined by a maqqef are joined by a hyphen: devar-Adonai.

Using the Reader

The bar at the foot of the screen sets the view: Interlinear (Hebrew with a gloss under each word), Hebrew Only, Dual (Hebrew and English side by side), Transliterate, and No Nikkud (the consonants alone, as in a Torah scroll). Beside it the buttons turn the chapter, and the chapter name between them opens the list of books. The two letters at the head of the page change the size of the text; the moon turns on night reading.

Tap any Hebrew word and its root scorecard opens: the root and its meaning, how many times that root occurs in each of the six volumes, its forms and glosses, and every reference. The scorecards are there so that you can test the translation for yourself: a word chosen for the Book of Mormon can be weighed against the same root in the Hebrew Bible, and where a root is rare or unattested the card says so.

Words in blue belong to the major themes of the Topical Guide; tap one to open the theme. Press and hold to select a run of words, then highlight it, add a note, copy or share it, or open its cross-references and glossary. Search finds Hebrew with or without vowel points, or English. The other five volumes open from the Standard Works page, and every scorecard reaches across all six.

The Hebrew text is an original translation into Biblical Hebrew by Chris Lambe; the English shown in Dual view is the 1920 edition. The reader itself was built with artificial intelligence under the translator's direction. The Brief Explanation in the front matter says more: About this edition →
